## Rules do not determine demand

Bitcoin's current consensus rules limit new issuance through a declining block subsidy. The allowed subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks; calendar estimates are approximate. Transaction fees paid to miners transfer existing bitcoin and are not new issuance. A miner cannot unilaterally issue extra coins that validating nodes accept. Changing consensus rules requires people to adopt compatible changes; software is maintained by people, rather than being a law of nature.

Limited supply is a monetary property, not a promise of increasing purchasing power. Demand, competition, regulation, market liquidity, security and expectations affect what others will exchange for sats. Lost coins also make usable supply different from theoretical issuance. A long time horizon can allow more flexibility, but it does not guarantee a profitable sale or recovery from loss.

## Match the obligation, not a slogan

A retirement goal decades away, rent due next week and a cooperative's member withdrawals have different timing and legal obligations. Document the expense currency, minimum acceptable outcome, flexibility and who depends on it. Consider debt, essential needs, income instability and the ability to withstand loss. Avoid borrowing on the assumption that future Bitcoin gains will service the loan. Debt obligations can remain when an asset falls.

Diversification spreads exposure to different failure sources; it does not guarantee gains or remove all risk. Three Bitcoin apps can share one asset-price risk. A Bitcoin salary, business revenue and savings balance can all suffer during the same downturn. Different custodians may still depend on the same banking partner, phone number or cloud account. Count underlying risks, not logos.

## Case: a goal measured in purchasing power

Fictional Noor has a future training bill initially priced at 12,000 local units. Her example resources are 8,000 accessible currency units and Bitcoin quoted at 4,000. Assume no returns on the currency balance. The bill rises 10% to 13,200; Bitcoin falls 50% to 2,000. Resources total 10,000 before costs, so the shortfall is **3,200**. This is not an allocation recommendation: the chosen amounts expose the mismatch for discussion.

Now suppose the currency account is inaccessible for a week and the bill is due tomorrow. Total assets and immediate resources differ. Recalculate under an income loss, an additional essential expense and an unavailable custodian. Do not average away a shortfall that occurs at the actual deadline. A plan should state what can change: the date, expense, income assumptions or arrangement. Some goals cannot safely tolerate asset-price risk.

## Sovereignty is a set of trade-offs

Holding keys can reduce dependence on account permission. You still rely on usable software, communications, safe physical space, willing counterparties and relevant law. Banking services can supply familiar payment rails, credit and dispute processes; Bitcoin can offer a different settlement option. They can interact rather than one replacing every institution. Public records can also expose financial activity; control of keys is not complete privacy.

## Try it

Write a one-page fictional goal policy: purpose, deadline, obligations, dependencies, loss/access tests and a review date. Set **decision questions**, not a recommended percentage to buy. Repeat Noor's example with Bitcoin rising 50%: resources become 14,000 and the pre-cost surplus over the higher bill is 800. Explain why that favorable case does not invalidate the 3,200 shortfall case or establish an expected return.
