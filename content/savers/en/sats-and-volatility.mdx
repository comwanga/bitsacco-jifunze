Juma hears that one bitcoin costs millions of shillings and decides it is not for him. But nobody has to buy a whole bitcoin, just as nobody has to buy a whole cow to drink milk.

## Sats: the small change of Bitcoin

One bitcoin is divided into 100,000,000 (one hundred million) <Term id="sats" />. Most people save and pay in sats.

Here is an example, using a made-up price that is **not** today's price:

| If 1 bitcoin costs | then 100 shillings buys |
| --- | --- |
| 10,000,000 KES | 1,000 sats |
| 5,000,000 KES | 2,000 sats |

The working: 100 ÷ 10,000,000 × 100,000,000 = 1,000 sats.

## Why the price moves

The price of bitcoin in shillings is set by people buying and selling every minute, all over the world. When more people want to buy than sell, the price rises; when more want to sell, it falls. News, fear and excitement all move it. Big, fast moves up and down are called <Term id="volatility" />.

Bitcoin's volatility is much higher than the shilling's. Over a week, a month or a year, the shilling value of your sats can fall a lot, as well as rise. Over the long run, nobody can promise which way it will go.

## Shillings and Bitcoin side by side

| | Shillings | Bitcoin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who issues it | Central Bank of Kenya | Nobody; fixed rules |
| How much can exist | Decided by the Central Bank | About 21 million, ever |
| Price in shillings | Always 1 | Changes every minute |
| Main risk | <Term id="inflation">Inflation</Term> slowly eats value | Big drops in the short term; losing your keys |

<Callout type="warning" title="Questions to ask before you save in Bitcoin">
When will I need this money? Could I wait if the price fell for a long time? Do I understand who holds the keys? Jifunze can't answer these for you, and nobody who promises a guaranteed return can be trusted.
</Callout>
