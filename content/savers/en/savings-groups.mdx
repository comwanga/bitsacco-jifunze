## Collective saving solves a coordination problem

People have long pooled small contributions to organize access to larger sums, support emergencies or pursue a shared goal. Regular meetings can encourage a habit and make records visible. Social trust can help, but social pressure can also silence a member who spots a problem. A savings group needs a way to question officials without losing access to the group.

The name does not tell you the whole arrangement. A Kenyan chama can have different purposes and rules. Related rotating arrangements appear under names such as arisan in Indonesia and pandero in Peru. Do not assume the same contribution schedule, borrowing permission, protection or legal category. Ask how money moves, who owns claims, and how losses are shared.

## Three patterns to recognize

| Pattern | Movement of contributions | Main question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rotating savings and credit association, or ROSCA | Each round's pot goes to one member; the cycle covers all members | Will members keep contributing after receiving their pot? |
| Accumulating savings and credit association | Contributions build a fund, sometimes making member loans | Who authorizes loans and bears default losses? |
| Village savings and loan association in CARE's model | Members accumulate savings, make governed loans and share out at cycle end; a social fund can have separate rules | How are shares, loans, social support and share-out calculated? |

These describe patterns, not every local implementation. A permanent cooperative institution has a different organizational and legal structure from a small self-managed group. A Bitcoin chat group does not become a cooperative merely by using shared keys.

## Case: six members, six rounds

In a fictional rotation, six members each contribute 500 local units per round. The pot is **3,000**. Across six rounds, every member contributes **3,000** and receives one **3,000** pot, assuming everyone pays and there are no fees or losses. The early recipient gains earlier access, not an automatic investment profit. Later recipients wait and bear the risk of earlier recipients failing to continue.

If the first recipient stops paying after round one, five later pots are each short by 500 unless the rules provide a workable response. Total missed future contributions are **2,500**. Taking larger contributions from everyone else is a new decision, not money created by the rotation. Inflation may also mean equal nominal pots buy different amounts at different times.

An accumulating group instead retains contributions. It must track each member's entitlement and any outstanding loans. A loan balance is not cash ready for withdrawal; expected interest is not earned cash before repayment. Keep emergency support separate if its rules do not create a repayable loan.

## Decide what Bitcoin would change

Bitcoin can be an optional payment or treasury asset, but a local-currency payout promise still needs local-currency resources. Price changes, fees, custody and conversion add tasks. A group may reasonably keep its present arrangement or run a paper experiment. It should not expose essential contributions just because one official is enthusiastic.

## Try it

Draw the six-round schedule and mark who receives each pot. Remove the first recipient's last five contributions and calculate every later pot. **Worked check:** each becomes 2,500 and the cycle misses 2,500 in total. Draft one question about default, one about payout order and one about how a member can challenge the records. Use invented members only.
